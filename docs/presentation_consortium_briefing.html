The design

The model travels. The data never does.

  • Every hospital keeps its images on its own machines, behind its own firewall. Nothing is copied to a central store, because there is no central store.
  • What moves is the model — a file of numbers. It contains no images, no reports and no patient details.
  • Each hospital improves the model on its own patients, then passes it to the next hospital in the ring.
  • A coordination server in Dresden decides whose turn it is. It never receives data and never does the learning itself.
That last point is what makes this a swarm. In most collaborative AI there is a central server that collects everyone's contributions. Here there is not — the hospitals hand the model directly to one another.

The design

What actually happens during a training round

The unit of work

One hospital, one turn with the model. Eight turns make a round. The August benchmark ran twenty rounds — 160 turns in all, roughly two hours each, spread over two days.

Why it takes so long

The hospitals are very different sizes, and a round is only finished when the slowest one has finished. A site with eighteen thousand scans and a site with a hundred and ninety are both taking a full turn.

The achievement · why it matters

The smaller the hospital, the more it gains

This is the case for the consortium existing, stated in one line: a hospital that trains alone is limited by the patients it happens to have seen.

What a small site sees on its own

Barcelona has 190 scans and no benign cases at all. Guildford has 351. Nijmegen has 3,608 scans but only 2 benign among them. A model trained on any of these learns whatever that hospital's referral pattern happens to look like, and mistakes it for how breast MRI works.

What the same site sees in the swarm

All 34,462 scans, from six countries, six scanner manufacturers and eight different ways of running a breast-MRI service — without a single image being copied anywhere. Every site takes the same finished model home.

And it is not only the small sites. Aachen holds more than half the data and still ends up with a better model by taking part. Being the largest contributor is not the same as having seen enough.

What the work actually was

Most of the effort went into making it survive the real world

The learning itself was rarely the hard part. Hospital IT is eight different networks, eight security policies and eight sets of hardware, and any one of them can end a two-day run.

  • 101 runs since March, 59 of them involving hospital sites.
  • Every distinct way a run can fail has been named, written up and given an automatic check, so it is caught before the next run rather than during it.
  • Development moved onto our own machines, so partner hardware is used only for real benchmark runs.

How we read the results

A number without its denominator is not a result

Until recently, each hospital's results had to be collected by logging into that hospital by hand. They now come back to the coordinator automatically — and the first thing they showed was a trap worth explaining to everyone.

HospitalCasesNo lesionBenignMalignantAccuracyActually useful?
Nijmegen8968880899.1 %0.417 — worse than guessing
Utrecht1,5571,3611861087.4 %0.698

Why 99 % means nothing here

Of Nijmegen's 896 cases, 888 have no lesion. A model that simply answers "no lesion" every single time scores 99 % — and finds no cancer at all. Reported on its own, that figure would make Nijmegen look like the strongest centre in the consortium.

This has already misled us once

Utrecht was described as performing materially worse than other centres. That came from an average across three classes in which one class had just two cases. Set that class aside and Utrecht moves 0.602 → 0.717, Cambridge 0.710 → 0.908.

None of this reflects on any hospital's data. It reflects on how we were summarising it. Every figure now travels with the counts behind it, and we no longer rank centres on an average where a class has only a handful of cases.

New this quarter

Two new capabilities — and one useful negative result

Choosing what to label next

Labelling breast MRI is expensive, so we tested whether the model can point at the cases worth a radiologist's time.

It cannot — yet. Asking the model for the cases it is least sure about found fewer malignant cases than picking at random: 19 against 22 out of a 100-case budget.

The diagnostic explains why, and it is worth knowing: the model is most confident on benign cases, which are the rarest. Its confidence does not track whether it is right, so asking it what it is unsure about points in the wrong direction. Confidence has to be fixed before this technique can help.

Putting a number on privacy

Until now we could say the design protects data. We can now say by how much, on the standard scale used by regulators.

Adding measured statistical noise to each hospital's contribution buys a formal guarantee. Over a twenty-round run:

  • a moderate guarantee needs a noise level of 2.4
  • a strong one needs 6.7
  • the strictest commonly cited standard needs 18.1

More noise costs accuracy, so this is now a decision the consortium can take deliberately instead of by accident.

What the privacy guarantee covers. It hides whether a hospital took part in a round. Hiding whether an individual patient was in a hospital's data is a further step, and we are not claiming it yet.

What we need from partners

Two things, and neither is technical

1 · Let sites return per-case predictions, not just summaries
What would be sent back: a row number, the correct answer, and the model's three probabilities. No case identifier and no patient identifier — the row number is a position within your own validation list and means nothing outside it. No images. Nothing new is computed: this is what your site already writes to its own disk during a normal training run.

Why we need it

  • Regional fine-tuning is currently blocked on physically moving a 722 MB model out of two hospitals. This removes that need entirely.
  • A fair statistical comparison between hospitals needs case-by-case pairing. Summary figures cannot provide it.
  • The regulatory testing node due next year requires it regardless.

What we are asking of you

  • Confirmation that this is acceptable under your local data agreement
  • It stays off unless your site switches it on — the decision is yours, per site
  • Nothing technical: no new software, no reconfiguration
  • Please keep your client online so active-learning tests can run
